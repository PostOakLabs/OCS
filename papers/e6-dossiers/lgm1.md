# Case Dossier: LGM-1 (first pulsar, 1967) — positive control

Purpose note: this dossier is raw material for a later WU that will apply the Paper E model-selection framework to a historical case where the "true" answer is already known (rotating neutron star). No scoring or framework judgment is performed here. The dossier is organized to let a later pass reconstruct, epoch by epoch, exactly which hypotheses were on the table *before* the correct answer existed, so the framework can be tested for whether it would have registered a strong non-null result in Nov 1967–early 1968 and then correctly collapsed onto the pulsar explanation once it entered the menu (Feb–May 1968).

## Observational data as published

Source: Hewish, A., Bell, S. J., Pilkington, J. D. H., Scott, P. F., Collins, R. A. (1968), "Observation of a Rapidly Pulsating Radio Source," *Nature* 217, 709–713 (24 February 1968). Received by *Nature* 9 February 1968 (submission date per Bell Burnell's own account below).

- Discovery telescope: the Interplanetary Scintillation Array, Mullard Radio Astronomy Observatory, Cambridge, built for a scintillation survey of compact radio sources (quasars) at 81.5 MHz, operated by Hewish's group with Bell as the PhD student running the daily chart-recorder analysis.
- First anomalous "scruff" noted on chart recordings in the source's data as early as August 1967 (per Bell Burnell's own account, below); recurred at the same sidereal time on successive days, which ruled out man-made terrestrial interference tied to solar/civil time and pointed to a fixed celestial position.
- A fast chart recorder was installed at the telescope (~late October/early November 1967) to resolve the time structure of the scruff, which the standard slow recorder could not do.
- The signal was captured on the fast recorder at the end of November 1967 (Bell Burnell dates this precisely to 28 November 1967 in most secondary chronologies built from the Cambridge logs; her own essay, quoted below, says only "the end of November '67").
- As published in Hewish et al. 1968: a series of pulses, period 1.337 s (commonly cited to high precision from later timing as 1.33730119 s; the discovery paper itself reports the period to the precision achieved by early-1968 timing runs, on the order of 1 part in 10^7–10^8 after several weeks of pulse-arrival-time monitoring), pulse duration approximately 0.3 s (width narrower than the period, i.e., a duty cycle well under 100%), an average power that fluctuated strongly from day to day (later understood as interstellar scintillation, not intrinsic to the source), and a source position fixed in sidereal (not solar) time, i.e., celestial rather than terrestrial or solar-system-local in the trivial sense.
- Object catalog name at publication: CP 1919 ("Cambridge Pulsar," right ascension 19h19m); now PSR B1919+21.
- By the time of submission, three further similar pulsating sources had been found by the same group at other positions on the sky (Bell Burnell's essay places the second around early December 1967, and two more around the Christmas period), which was itself evidentiary: multiple independent sources sharing the same class of signal weighed heavily against any single localized artificial or interference origin.

## Hypothesis menu — epoch by epoch

### Epoch 0: August–October 1967 (anomaly noted, pre-fast-recording)

Menu entertained, per Bell Burnell's own later first-person account (S. Jocelyn Bell Burnell, "Little Green Men, White Dwarfs or Pulsars?", after-dinner address, Eighth Texas Symposium on Relativistic Astrophysics, published *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences* 302, 685–689, 1977; reprinted *Cosmic Search* 1(1), 1979, http://www.bigear.org/vol1no1/burnell.htm):

- Man-made terrestrial interference / instrumental artifact — considered and disfavored early because the "scruff" recurred at a fixed sidereal (not solar/civil) time, which is the signature of a celestial rather than terrestrial or human-schedule-linked source.
- No specific astrophysical model yet proposed; the signal's fine time structure was unresolved because the standard chart recorder's paper speed was too slow to show it.

### Epoch 1: late November – December 1967 (fast-recording capture, initial internal investigation, pre-publication)

Bell Burnell's own account of the menu actually held internally by the Cambridge group at this stage:

- **Little Green Men (LGM) / extraterrestrial artificial signal.** Direct quote: "We did not really believe that we had picked up signals from another civilization, but obviously the idea had crossed our minds and we had no proof that it was an entirely natural radio emission." The group tested this specifically by checking for Doppler shift in the pulse period that would be expected "as the little green men on their planet orbited their sun" — i.e., they ran an actual quantitative orbital-Doppler test against the ET hypothesis, not just an intuition. (Hewish independently corroborates taking the possibility seriously enough to raise it in contemporaneous correspondence — see Gap note 1 below on the letter to Lynden-Bell, which this dossier could not independently verify from a primary-source text.)
- **Terrestrial interference / instrumental effects, specifically:** radar signals reflected off the Moon into the telescope; artificial satellites in unusual orbits; anomalous effects from a large corrugated metal building south of the telescope. All explicitly "considered and eliminated" per Bell Burnell's account. Independently, Scott and Collins re-observed the pulsations with a second, separate telescope and receiver chain, which eliminated single-instrument artifacts.
- **Natural but unidentified celestial source, class undetermined** — held open pending the above eliminations.

The LGM hypothesis was abandoned as the leading candidate specifically when a second, independent pulsating source was found at a different sky position within weeks of the first (Bell Burnell: "It was very unlikely that two lots of little green men would both choose the same, improbable frequency, and the same time, to try signalling to the same planet Earth"). This is a dated, in-house model-comparison judgment, not a later retrospective simplification: multiplicity of independent sources was treated by the discoverers themselves as decisive evidence against the artificial-beacon class, prior to any neutron-star theory being available.

### Epoch 2: publication, Hewish et al. 1968, *Nature* 217, 709 (24 February 1968; received 9 February 1968)

The published menu, as it appears in the discovery paper itself (per secondary description consistent across multiple independent sources — see Gap note 2 on direct paper text access):

- Terrestrial/interference origin: explicitly ruled out in the paper via the same eliminations described above, now presented as settled fact for publication.
- Natural celestial radio source, physical mechanism proposed as **radial pulsation (oscillation) of a compact star — either a white dwarf or a neutron star.** This is presented in the paper as the favored natural explanation at time of publication. Multiple modern historical sources describe this as "Hewish conceived of a natural explanation — a neutron star" during the paper-writing stage (mid-January 1968), but the mechanism proposed was **pulsation/vibration of the star, not rotation.** Rotation as the driving mechanism is not the Hewish et al. 1968 proposal; it is the Gold/Pacini proposal that supersedes it in Epoch 3.
- The paper does not, on the available record, present the LGM/artificial-signal hypothesis as a formal candidate in the text (it appears to have been an internally discussed and internally discarded possibility, not something carried into the published hypothesis menu) — see Gap note 2.

### Epoch 3: rotating-neutron-star hypothesis enters (spring 1968)

Two distinct, non-simultaneous theoretical contributions, which must not be conflated:

- **Pacini, F. (1967), "Energy Emission from a Neutron Star," *Nature* 216, 567–568 (11 November 1967).** This paper **predates** the pulsar discovery's public announcement (and very nearly predates the fast-recorder detection itself, which per Bell Burnell's account also falls at the end of November 1967). Pacini's paper is a theoretical prediction, made independent of and prior to knowledge of CP 1919, that a rapidly rotating, strongly magnetized neutron star could convert rotational energy into electromagnetic/particle emission — motivated by the energy-supply problem for the Crab Nebula, not by any pulsating radio source (none was yet known to Pacini in Nov 1967 in any published sense; the Cambridge detection was not yet public). Pacini's paper is therefore historically prior but was **not** the explanation initially connected to CP 1919 by the Cambridge group at time of the Hewish et al. 1968 publication.
- **Gold, T. (1968), "Rotating Neutron Stars as the Origin of the Pulsating Radio Sources," *Nature* 218, 731–732 (25 May 1968).** This is the paper that explicitly connects rotation of a neutron star (as opposed to radial pulsation) to the newly announced pulsating radio sources, proposing the rotating-beacon/lighthouse mechanism and making falsifiable predictions (e.g., gradual spin-down) that were subsequently confirmed. This is the paper that directly supersedes the Hewish et al. 1968 "pulsation of a compact star" proposal for the pulsating-radio-source class specifically.

Gap note (see below): this dossier could not independently confirm from primary-source full text whether Gold's paper explicitly cites or credits Pacini 1967, or whether Gold arrived at the rotation mechanism independently; multiple modern summaries describe the two as "independently" linking rotation to pulsars, but the two papers differ in date (Nov 1967 vs. May 1968) and in target phenomenon (Crab energy budget vs. newly discovered pulsating radio sources), so they are not a single simultaneous event and should not be cited as such.

### Epoch 4: resolution / consensus

- The Crab Nebula pulsar (NP 0532), discovered by Staelin and Reifenstein (1968) and shown by Comella, Craft, Lovelace, Sutton, and Tyler (1969, *Science*) to have a period of 33 ms with a measurable spin-down rate consistent with the rotational-energy-loss mechanism Pacini and Gold had proposed, is broadly credited (per secondary historical summaries) as the observation that moved the rotating-neutron-star model from "a" candidate to consensus, because the very short period (33 ms) was difficult to reconcile with radial pulsation models for known stellar compact-object masses, while it was straightforwardly consistent with rotation. This dossier flags the Comella et al. 1969 citation as unverified against the original *Science* text — see Gap note 3.
- By this point (1969), the rotating-neutron-star (pulsar) model had displaced both the radial-pulsation-of-a-compact-star proposal of Hewish et al. 1968 and the residual LGM possibility, which had already been effectively abandoned internally by the Cambridge group before publication (Epoch 1) once a second source was found.

## Resolution

The case resolved in two analytically separate steps that this dossier keeps distinct for the later scoring pass:
1. **Artificial-signal (LGM) hypothesis collapse**, achieved internally by the discovery team itself within weeks of first detection (Nov–Dec 1967), on the strength of (a) a null Doppler test for orbital motion and (b) discovery of a second, independent pulsating source at a different position — both dated to before the paper was even submitted (9 February 1968), and therefore before any public announcement or outside input.
2. **Mechanism resolution among natural-source candidates** (radial pulsation of a white dwarf or neutron star, vs. rotation of a neutron star), which took from publication (24 Feb 1968) through Gold's paper (25 May 1968) and the Crab pulsar timing result (Comella et al. 1969) to settle observationally in favor of rotation.
