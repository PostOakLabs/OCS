A Radio and X-ray Bound on Bondi-fed Accretion onto the Candidate Intermediate-Mass Black Hole in Omega Centauri, with an Infrared Consistency Check

Abstract

Three deep non-detections constrain accretion onto the candidate intermediate-mass black hole (IMBH) in Omega Centauri: 170 hours of ATCA radio imaging at 1.1 μJy rms (Mahida et al. 2026), JWST imaging with no accretion-like point source (Chen et al. 2026), and a 291-ks Chandra limit LX ≤ 1.6×1030 erg s−1 (Haggard et al. 2013). We build a forward model from the Bondi radiative efficiency εB ≡ Lbol/(ṀBc2), the mass and eight shared nuisance parameters, and report the 95 per cent credible upper limit ε95 for three accretion-flow families. For a radiatively inefficient flow, ε95 = 3.0×10−10 at 8,200 M☉ and 1.1×10−11 at 4×104 M☉. These limits are conditional on the fundamental plane; without the radio leg they relax to 1.4×10−8 and 8.1×10−10. The infrared leg never binds, so this is a radio-plus-X-ray bound with an infrared consistency check. Against a natural outflow-suppressed hot flow at a 47 Tucanae-like gas density, the data reject 85, 77 and 99 per cent of the parameter space at 8,200 M☉, the pulsar-timing cap of 6,000 M☉ and 4×104 M☉ (28, 20 and 76 per cent without radio). At one tenth of that density, just below the 0.03–0.08 cm−3 wind-fed estimate, the figures fall to 57, 47 and 94 per cent (4, 2 and 32). The verdict applies the lowest branch of an efficiency fit three to four decades below that branch’s upper edge, and a pulsar dispersion-measure determination of the density is foreground-limited until the sight-line scatter is modelled down. Block-by-block analysis of the radio campaign excludes 100 μJy, 2-hr flares recurring faster than every 31 hr, and leaves 1 mJy half-hour flares unconstrained beyond 3.4 days.

1. Introduction

Omega Centauri hosts the nearest strong IMBH candidate: fast-moving stars inside the central arcsecond require an enclosed dark mass ≳ 8,200 M☉ (Häberle et al. 2024), while pulsar-timing and kinematic modeling favor an extended dark remnant component of ~2–3×105 M☉ and cap any point mass at ≲ 6,000 M☉ at 3σ (Bañares-Hernández et al. 2025), a tension sharpened rather than resolved by the current 19-pulsar timing set (Colom i Bernadich et al. 2026). The center is electromagnetically silent at the depths quantified below. The radio silence has a two-decade history: upper limits of ~100 μJy at GHz frequencies (Maccarone et al. 2005), a 2.5σ near-center excursion flagged as worth deeper follow-up (Lu & Kong 2011), JVLA limits of 1.5–2.1 μJy beam−1 in three other clusters that set the methodological template for radio IMBH searches (Strader et al. 2012), the 50-cluster MAVERIC survey null (Tremou et al. 2018), and now the deepest radio image of any globular cluster, 170 hours with ATCA reaching 1.1 μJy rms at 7.25 GHz with no source at any proposed center and the nearest 5σ source 13 arcseconds away (Mahida et al. 2026). The Lu & Kong (2011) excursion does not recur at a factor ~6 deeper in rms; the 2.5σ peak they flagged would have registered at ≳ 14σ in the new image, which retires that thread. In the infrared, JWST NIRCam and MIRI imaging of the core shows no point source with an accretion-like spectral energy distribution (Chen et al. 2026); in X-rays, the combined 291-ks Chandra exposure gives LX ≤ 1.6×1030 erg s−1, which Haggard et al. (2013) translated to accretion efficiencies spanning 10−5–10−9 across the 3×103–3×104 M☉ mass window they consider. Their abstract rounds that span to 10−6–10−8; the wider figure is the one their text states.

Chen et al. (2026) place their infrared limits alongside the radio limits and note that the radio is the more restrictive at the high-mass end (their Figure 7). That comparison is qualitative: each band’s limit is quoted under its own model assumptions, at its own fiducial mass, with no shared error budget. The three limits constrain three different quantities, a radio flux routed through a jet or fundamental-plane relation, a band-limited infrared luminosity, and a band-limited X-ray luminosity, and all three depend on the same unmeasured gas density, the same contested black-hole mass, and the same distance. Multiplying or stacking them as if independent would overstate the joint constraint; quoting any of them at a single mass begs the question the mass tension leaves open.

This paper supplies the missing joint analysis, and in doing so surfaces a result the single-band treatments could not state: the combined data are now deep enough to exclude most of the parameter space of a natural Bondi-fed hot flow across the contested mass range. Section 2 fixes the observational inputs; Section 3 the forward model, the likelihoods, and the three accretion-flow families; Section 4 the stellar-wind estimate of the dominant nuisance; Section 5 the posterior curves, the natural-flow comparison, and the prior-sensitivity budget; Section 6 the duty-cycle loophole; Section 7 forecasts; and Section 8 what the bound adjudicates. Appendix A collects every unit conversion and convention; Appendix B retains a simpler threshold-inversion construction as a transparent cross-check, and Appendix C records the version history. The Monte Carlo script, its fixed seed, and its full output accompany the paper.

3. Method

3.1 Definitions and conventions

Let ṀB be the Bondi capture rate from gas at rest in the cluster core,

ṀB = 4πλ (GM)2ρgas/cs3, λ(γ=5/3) = 0.25 (Bondi 1952), (1)

with ρgas = μempne and μe = 1.17 for an ionized plasma of solar-like composition. Gas in hydrostatic equilibrium shares the cluster’s rest frame, and the hole’s Brownian velocity, σ(m*/M)1/2 ~ 0.1 km s−1, is negligible against cs, so no bulk-velocity term enters the denominator; this is the convention of the comparison literature (Strader et al. 2012; Tremou et al. 2018; Haggard et al. 2013). A variant that treats the stellar velocity dispersion as a turbulent-velocity proxy in the denominator, the convention of Paper E’s fuel budget, is evaluated in Appendix A; it lowers ṀB by a factor ≃ 1.9 net of the eigenvalue and weakens every limit by the same factor.

We define the Bondi radiative efficiency

εB ≡ Lbol/(ṀBc2), (2)

the bolometric radiative output per unit of captured rest-mass energy, the quantity the data constrain directly. It factors as εB = η fB, with fB the fraction of captured gas reaching the horizon and η the radiative efficiency of the arriving flow. In natural hot flows the inflow declines inward, Ṁ(R) ∝ Rs, with 0 ≤ s ≤ 1 admissible on general grounds (Blandford & Begelman 1999) and modeled values spanning s ≃ 0.3–0.8 (Yuan & Narayan 2014); we adopt s ∈ [0.3, 0.5] as this paper’s working sub-range within that modeled span. With rB = GM/cs2 and the gravitational radius rg = GM/c2, so that rB/rg = (c/cs)2 and the bracket depends on cs and s alone,

fB = (rB/rg)−s ∈ [10−4.4, 10−2.6] for s ∈ [0.3, 0.5], (3)

consistent with Paper E’s fiducial s ≃ 0.3 suppression of ~500 at 2×104 M☉. Published per-band efficiencies use other conventions: Mahida et al. (2026) bound a model-specific accretion fraction (≲ 4×10−3), Haggard et al. (2013) an efficiency under fixed gas density and mass (10−5–10−9 over their stated mass window). Appendix A prints the conversion table; none of those numbers is εB, and no direct numerical comparison should be made without it.

The gas density is the weakest input and it multiplies everything, so its treatment is spread across three places: the prior here (a 47 Tucanae analogy, since ω Cen has no direct ionized-gas measurement; the one ω Cen-specific figure, ne = 1.94 cm−3 from the pulsar dispersion-measure scatter, should in its authors’ words be regarded as an upper limit, since their sodium correction removes only ~20 per cent of that scatter and, in their reading, likely underestimates a foreground responsible for much of the rest (§ VI.1.3); it is not used; Wang et al. 2025), the wind budget of Section 4, which estimates the local supply, and the sensitivity budget of Section 5.3. Mahida et al. (2026) adopt a gas density of 0.2 ± 0.1 cm−3 following Strader et al. (2012). In the provenance audit of Paper I this is a gas number density, the quantity Strader et al. write ρ and distinct from the electron densities they measure, and Tremou et al. (2018) state that it corresponds to an electron density of 0.1 cm−3; labelling it ne carries a factor of two that the symbol hides. The 0.2 originates in Freire et al. (2001), who found the dispersion measures of four millisecond pulsars in M15 consistent with a homogeneous plasma of about 0.2 cm−3, as an alternative to the original analysis of the same pulsars (Anderson 1993), which attributed their variation to the Galactic foreground and set only an upper limit of 5 cm−3 on the core electron density. (Both Freire et al. (2001) and Mahida et al. (2026) cite the thesis as Anderson (1992); the Caltech record dates it 1993.) The value’s later agreement with the 47 Tuc measurement (Abbate et al. 2018) is the analogy on which the median used here also rests, so it does not test that median independently. Every predicted flux in the forward model scales linearly with neεB, so the data constrain the product, and the normalization of every curve in this paper is conditional on the ne prior. Every part of the εB curve depends on the 47 Tuc analogy; what can be done, and is done below, is to bound the analogy’s plausible range astrophysically and to show the answer across that range.

The distance prior is centred on the paper set’s fiducial of 5.49 kpc (Appendix A.1), the value Chen et al. (2026) adopt and within 0.1 per cent of the kinematic 5.494 ± 0.061 kpc of Mahida et al. (2026); its width, 0.05 kpc, is that of the 5.43 ± 0.05 kpc of Baumgardt & Vasiliev (2021), on which versions up to 2.3 were centred. A more distant source is fainter at fixed luminosity, so every predicted flux falls and the limits loosen. Moving the centre from 5.43 to 5.49 kpc raises every ε95 by 1.2 to 3.1 per cent (median 2.4), a mixture of the (5.49/5.43)2 = 1.022 of the luminosity-derived legs and the 1.04 of the radio leg, which enters through the fundamental plane’s 0.60 index as (5.49/5.43)2/0.60, and lowers the exclusion fractions by up to 0.0033.

3.3 Forward model and likelihoods

For each trial (εB, M) and nuisance draw θ: Lbol = εB ṀB(M,θ) c2. The predicted observables are

fXpred = fXLbol/(4πd2), (4) (νLν)predIR = fIRLbol, (5) log LRpred = 0.60 log(k fXLbol) + 0.78 log(M/M☉) + 7.33 + δFP, (6)

where k = 0.610 converts the 0.5–7 keV band fraction to the 2–10 keV band the fundamental plane is calibrated in (power law, Γ = 2; Haggard et al.’s own Γ = 2.3 gives k = 0.462 and is carried as a ladder row in Appendix A), and δFP ~ 𝒩(0, 0.88 dex) is the plane’s intrinsic scatter as a latent variable. The plane is used only in the forward direction, the direction it was fit in (Merloni et al. 2003); no inverse regression is performed, which removes the calibration-problem objection that attaches to algebraic inversion of a scattered forward relation (Plotkin et al. 2012). The likelihood is the product of six Gaussians on the measured values: central radio flux density 0 ± 1.1 μJy, central X-ray flux 0 ± fX,lim/1.645, and four infrared band-peak luminosities 0 ± σj from Table 1, the infrared terms rescaled to the drawn distance from Chen et al.’s 5.49 kpc. Where a band’s published product is a limit rather than a measured value, a Gaussian whose σ is that limit divided by the quantile of the confidence the source itself states is the reconstruction we adopt for the two legs published as limits, and Appendix A measures what it costs; the data-availability section lists exactly those numbers as the ones that would replace it, and the machinery ingests them unchanged.

The posterior at each M is computed as the plain Monte Carlo mean of the likelihood over the 4×104 nuisance draws, which is an unweighted average because the draws come from the prior itself and no proposal reweighting is applied, on a log-spaced εB grid (10−14–10−2, log-uniform prior), and ε95 denotes the 95 per cent credible upper limit of the normalized posterior. Re-running the anchors under eight nuisance seeds, each with its own δ stream, puts the Monte Carlo spread of ε95 at 1.4 to 9.6 per cent of the mean across the three anchors and the three families, widest for the RIAF family and narrowest for the radio-free curve, and the spread of Pexcl at 0.13 to 0.88 percentage points. Both sit an order of magnitude under the analysis-choice ranges of Section 5.3. The seed of record sits at the low end of the ε95 range and the high end of the Pexcl range; the eight-seed minima and maxima ship in figs/fF_v4_seedspread.json. Upper limits from non-detections are sensitive to the prior’s lower cutoff; Section 5.3 quantifies that dependence (a factor of 9 to 40 when the floor rises from 10−14 to 10−11) and every quoted ε95 states its grid. This likelihood product, not a distribution of inverted thresholds, is what Paper E’s Bayesian adjudication machinery consumes; the marginal likelihood over εB ships as a machine-readable table with the source.

3.4 Accretion-flow families

A single SED bracket cannot serve all flows, and under a common bracket for fX and fIR the X-ray leg dominates the infrared leg in every draw, leaving JWST unconstraining in this band, which would make a three-band claim cosmetic. 3.5 The natural-flow expectation and its efficiency law

The limit curve becomes a verdict only against a prediction, and the prediction is εnat = ηADAF(ṁhor) fB, with ṁhor = fBṀB/ṀEdd the rate reaching the horizon in Eddington units. Xie & Yuan (2012) give η for hot flows as a piecewise power law,

η(ṁ) = η0(ṁ/10−2)a, (7)

with (η0, a) tabulated per branch and per δ, the fraction of turbulent dissipation delivered directly to electrons. In the lowest branch, the one relevant here, a lies between 0.59 and 0.71 (0.65, 0.59, 0.69 and 0.71 at δ = 0.5, 0.1, 10−2 and 10−3; the 0.47 of the δ = 10−3 row belongs to the middle branch) and η0 runs from 0.065 to 1.58 across δ = 10−3 to 0.5. We adopt their Table 1 as printed and carry δ as an eighth nuisance, drawn uniformly over the four tabulated values, since no measurement of it exists for this object. Like the suppression index s, δ enters εnat and not the likelihood, so its whole influence runs through the comparison rather than through ε95.

A law linear in ṁ, η = 0.1 min(1, ṁ/10−2), the form used up to v1.5, is not a simplification of these fits: at the rates this problem reaches it is lower than any tabulated δ by one to three decades, because a = 1 falls away far faster than a ≃ 0.6. Using the fits raises the expectation band by about two decades and is the larger of the two reasons the verdict of Section 5 differs from v1.5 (Appendix C).

A caveat travels with the correction and is not removed by it. The horizon-scale rates here are ṁhor ≃ 10−9 to 10−6, with a median of 1.8×10−8 at the fast-star anchor, while the lowest branch of Xie & Yuan’s piecewise fit (their Table 1) is tabulated for ṁnet ≲ 1.6×10−5 to 9.4×10−5 depending on δ, with no lower edge printed. Every number in this paper’s expectation band therefore applies that branch below its upper edge, as the linear law it replaces also did: by 2.9 to 3.7 decades at the median rate (log10 of 1.6×10−5 and 9.4×10−5 over 1.8×10−8), and by 1.2 to 5.0 decades across the full range ṁhor = 10−9 to 10−6. The direction of the extrapolation matters for how the verdict should be read: continuing the power law downward gives the smallest η of any reading, so it is the choice least favourable to exclusion. Holding η flat at the value on the fit’s own upper edge, the opposite bracket, drives every exclusion fraction in Table 6 to 0.99 or above. The extrapolated law is what the headline numbers use, and the two rows below the rule in that table are the bracket.

Sgr A* offers one consistency point inside that gap. Taking a horizon accretion rate of order 10−8 M☉ yr−1 (Faraday-rotation limits of 2×10−9 to 2×10−7, Marrone et al. 2007) and a bolometric luminosity of about 1036 erg s−1 (Yuan & Narayan 2014), with ṀEdd = 10 LEdd/c2 ≃ 0.09 M☉ yr−1 for 4×106 M☉ (Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration 2022), gives ṁ ≃ 10−7 and η = L/(Ṁc2) ≃ 2×10−3. The lowest branch of Xie & Yuan continued to ṁ = 10−7 gives 9×10−4 at δ = 0.5 and 1.3×10−4 at δ = 0.1, so Sgr A* sits inside the ṁhor range of this paper, near the δ = 0.5 branch. Three limits apply to this point. The δ values of Xie & Yuan (2012) follow models calibrated on Sgr A*-type sources, so the agreement is not independent of the fit. It fixes one (ṁ, δ) point, so using it assumes that ω Cen shares the δ of Sgr A*. And at the median ṁhor of 1.8×10−8 it leaves a factor of 5.6 in ṁ (0.75 decade) to extrapolate. Figure 1 does not mark it.

3.6 Defining the exclusion fraction

Each natural-flow draw is a fully specified model: ε = εnat(θ) with that draw’s own nuisances. It predicts a flux in every observed band, and the measured data, zero central flux at the published σj, then have a joint chi-square under it, χ2 = Σb(predb/σb)2 over the six terms of Section 3.3, distributed as χ26 if that draw is the truth. We call a draw excluded when the data reject it at the 5 per cent level, and report

Pexcl = P[ p(χ2) < 0.05 ] (8)

over the nuisance draws, with its Monte Carlo standard error.

A statistic reported under the same name up to v1.5 (Appendix C) was a different quantity: the fraction of natural draws exceeding ε95, with ε95 itself marginalized over those same draws. That statistic compares a distribution against a scalar built from it, uses common random numbers on both sides, and has no coverage reading; it also produced the artefact that widening the density prior cost the verdict almost nothing, since a log-normal’s median does not move when its width does, so the exceedance count barely changed. Equation 8 avoids all three. It is per-draw, it uses the likelihood rather than a summary of it, and it never reads ε95, which is why the efficiency prior’s lower cutoff, a factor of 9 to 40 on ε95, cannot touch it. The old statistic is retained in Table 4 under its correct name, recomputed on independent draws so that the common random numbers are gone; it runs 0.03 to 0.04 above the posterior-predictive figure at the two lower anchors and reaches 1.00 at the high anchor where the posterior-predictive value is 0.99.

4. The stellar-wind gas supply

The ne prior needs an astrophysical anchor, and the cluster’s own stellar winds supply one. A recount of its inputs (figs/fF_calc7_windfloor.py) shows that the budget is a no-removal estimate; v1.5 and earlier used it as a floor, and the correction runs to two decades (Appendix C).

The chain is the standard one. Inside the influence radius rinfl = GM/σ2 = 0.39 pc of a 4×104 M☉ hole, a Plummer model of the cluster (M = 4×106 M☉, a = 3.78 pc) encloses 4.3×103 M☉ of stars, about 7.2×103 stars at a luminous-mass mean of 0.6 M☉. Of those, the Kroupa weight of the turnoff window is 0.035 and the fraction of a turnoff star’s main-sequence life spent above 103 L☉ is (0.35 + 0.07)/13 = 0.032, so the standing giant population inside rinfl is 8.1 stars, and 5.2 to 11.0 across the phase-lifetime and mean-mass variations tested. At 10−8 M☉ yr−1 each the aggregate injection is 8.1×10−8 M☉ yr−1, and if nothing removes it the steady state at an assumed outflow speed vout ~ 50 km s−1 is

ne ~ Ṁwind/(4πrinfl2 μempvout) ≃ 0.029 cm−3, (9)

with 0.018 to 0.039 cm−3 over the same variations, refilled on the crossing time rinfl/vout ~ 104 yr. The superseded count of 103 giants reproduces 3.5 cm−3 through the same arithmetic and was too large by a factor of 123, having counted the stars rather than the ones currently on the giant branch (Appendix C).

The recount changes three things.

The wind budget is an estimate. A no-removal steady state is the most gas the winds can sustain at a given outflow speed, but 0.029 cm−3 is not a ceiling: it is linear in the assumed vout ~ 50 km s−1, and an outflow slowed to the sound speed (vout ≈ cs) raises it to about 0.10 cm−3. Three estimates bracket the wind-fed density at 0.03–0.08 cm−3: this recount (0.029), the Haggard et al. (2013) wind budget (0.038, below), and the 0.0764 cm−3 upper edge of the ω Cen envelope in the gas-provenance audit of Paper I (its tier table). The retired ne ≥ 0.05 cm−3 floor sits a factor 1.7 above it, and the claims made for that floor are withdrawn (Appendix C). Section 5.3 now reports a truncation at ne ≥ 0.02 cm−3, inside the recounted range, and states what it costs rather than what it recovers.

The classical gas problem does not arise here. Globular clusters are gas-poorer than their stellar winds imply, and in 47 Tuc a removal agent, most plausibly millisecond-pulsar winds and heating, is required to explain it (Freire et al. 2001; Abbate et al. 2018). At 8 giants inside 0.39 pc there is little to remove: the wind supply and the 47 Tuc analogy value differ by a factor of 8 in the direction that needs no removal agent at all.

The recount bears on the prior’s centre. The 0.23 cm−3 median is imported from 47 Tuc, and ω Cen’s own giants inside the influence radius cannot supply it without a source this budget omits. An independent route reaches a similar figure: Haggard et al. (2013) adopt ne = 0.038 cm−3 for this cluster from a Pfahl–Rappaport wind budget, within 32 per cent of the recount here despite a different formalism and radius. Two readings survive. Either the central density is set by material the influence-radius wind budget does not track, in which case the 47 Tuc analogy stands and the prior is right, or it is set by the local supply, in which case the prior median is high by a factor of 3 to 8 (0.23 against 0.0764 to 0.029) and every limit in this paper is correspondingly loose. We do not collapse that into one number. The prior is left at its published centre so that the curves remain comparable with the comparison literature, the ne median/10 row of Table 5 is the quantitative price of the second reading, and Section 7 states that a pulsar dispersion-measure gradient is the measurement that would end the argument and that the present sight-line scatter keeps it out of reach until that scatter is modelled. On the second reading the density evidence sits at the weaker end of this paper’s exclusion range, which is the direction Table 6’s prior-shift row prices.

5.2 Which band binds which family

5.3.1 Which nuisance carries the spread

Table 5 varies analysis choices. The nuisance parameters can be interrogated separately, by re-running the same posterior with one of them held at its prior’s central value while the others are drawn, and again with only that one drawn and the others held (Figure 5). The two views answer different questions: the first asks how much of the limit’s looseness a given parameter is responsible for, the second asks how much looseness that parameter would produce on its own. Seven parameters appear; the eighth, δ, is absent for the same structural reason as s, since it enters εnat and not the likelihood, so freezing it cannot move ε95.

The answer is that the fundamental plane’s latent scatter carries the spread. Freezing δFP at zero tightens ε95 by a factor of 13 at 8,200 M☉ and 29 at 4×104 M☉; drawing it alone, with everything else fixed, reproduces a limit 18 and 61 times looser than the fully frozen case. No other parameter comes within an order of magnitude. The gas density is second and is a distant second in this accounting: freezing it tightens ε95 by 21 to 25 per cent, and drawing it alone loosens the frozen limit by a factor 1.6 to 2.4. The X-ray band fraction contributes 8 to 10 per cent, the sound speed under 1 per cent, and the distance nothing measurable, as expected from a 1 per cent prior. The infrared band fraction moves the RIAF limit by 0.4 per cent in the opposite direction, which is the signature of a leg that never binds in this family.

The distance prior and the suppression index are inert here, for structural reasons worth naming. The distance prior is narrow enough that its 1 per cent width cannot register against the plane’s 0.88 dex. The suppression index s does not enter the likelihood at all: it sets the natural-flow expectation εnat against which the limit is compared, not the limit itself, so its entire influence in this paper runs through Pexcl and the grey band of Figure 1 rather than through ε95.

This does not contradict Section 3.2’s statement that the gas density owns the result. The two statements are about different things. Within the stated priors, the plane’s scatter is what widens the posterior; the density’s danger is in the choice of prior centre, which no freezing test can expose, and which Table 5’s prior-shift rows price at a factor ~6. A reader who distrusts the plane has the radio-free curve; a reader who distrusts the density analogy has the shift rows and the wind recount of Section 4, which points the same way.

6. The duty-cycle loophole

The three inputs sample time very differently, and Mahida et al. (2026)’s Table 1 makes the radio sampling explicit: 25 observing blocks across three ATCA projects, 2010 January 22 to 2024 December 27, summing to 177.19 hr on source (CX556, 20 blocks, 147.09 hr; C2877, 3 blocks, 11.79 hr; C2158, 2 blocks, 18.31 hr). Those rows exceed the ~170 hr of the paper’s abstract and the 172 hr of its Section II.3; we report the sum as printed and leave the reconciliation to that paper, noting that prose totals quoted after flagging would run below a raw table sum rather than above it. Chandra contributes four exposures in two epochs twelve years apart, JWST a single epoch.

Two consequences follow on different timescales. For variability slower than the sampled span, the limits above apply to the time-average δ εon of a flow active a fraction δ of the time at εon, so εon ≲ ε95/δ; the radio image is a weighted combination over the full fourteen-year span, so this is the operative statement there. For variability faster than a block, the source must be off during every epoch to escape, which for N ≃ 30 independent epochs (25 radio blocks, four Chandra exposures, one JWST visit) gives a miss probability (1−δ)N reaching 50 per cent only near δ ≈ 0.02. This order-of-magnitude estimate counts every block or exposure as one independent trial regardless of its duration relative to the assumed flare timescale τ; Section 6.1’s block-by-block treatment does not carry that assumption and supersedes this estimate in rigor. The excluded region is therefore the band εon > ε95/δ down to a few per cent duty cycle, and effectively nothing below that: a flow that spends 99 per cent of its time off is constrained only by the luck of the sampling. This is the standard loophole of every quiescent-limit paper, here made explicit because Paper E’s tidal-disruption arithmetic predicts exactly such intermittency on 107-yr recurrence with 10−4 duty, far below the reach of any current sampling; nothing in this paper’s nulls bears on that channel, in either direction.

The block list supports a stronger statement than a miss probability, because each block is an image in its own right. 7. Forecasts

Three measurements would harden or move the curves, in descending order of leverage per unit effort.

A measured core density. The 19-pulsar timing set (Colom i Bernadich et al. 2026) makes an ω Cen dispersion-measure gas measurement possible in principle for the first time, by the method introduced on M15’s pulsars (Anderson 1993) and since used to measure 47 Tuc (Freire et al. 2001; Abbate et al. 2018); the fit below shows that the present sight-line scatter prevents it in practice. A second caveat, that DM gradients constrain the column through the pulsar volume rather than the density at the hole, is real and enters as geometry. The forecast gain on ε95 itself is modest (a ±10 per cent density measurement tightens the baseline by ~22 per cent, since the 0.5-dex prior is already informative). A measurement converts every curve from analogy-conditional to measured, removes the largest single objection to the enterprise, and, if the density comes in low, legitimately weakens the bounds when the conditions are tested.

Table 9 is the requirement that forecast implies, computed from the published timing set. All 19 pulsars have measured dispersion measures; eight have them from full timing solutions with formal uncertainties of 5×10−5 to 1.3×10−3 pc cm−3, and five of those eight also have a measured first DM derivative. Timing precision is not the obstacle.

Geometry sets what a dispersion-measure gradient can reach. Gas filling the cluster core, a uniform sphere of the catalogue core radius 2.37′ (Harris 2010), or 3.78 pc at 5.49 kpc, imprints a central-chord excess of 0.76 pc cm−3 at ne = 0.1 cm−3, and 13 of the 19 sight lines pass inside it. Gas confined to the influence radius of the hole, the 0.4 pc region the Bondi rate is actually about, imprints 0.08 pc cm−3 and no published sight line passes through it: the innermost pulsar, H at 0.56′, projects to 0.89 pc. A DM determination therefore measures the core-filling component and reaches the Bondi-scale density only through an assumed profile, which is the caveat named above, now with a number attached.

The sight-line scatter sets how well it can be measured. The 19 published DMs span 94.3 to 102.6 pc cm−3 with an r.m.s. of 2.9 pc cm−3, three orders of magnitude above the per-pulsar measurement precision and four times the signal a core-filling ne = 0.1 cm−3 would produce. Fitting the chord template plus a free foreground constant to the 19 measurements by least squares returns an amplitude of −0.33 ± 0.23 cm−3, consistent with zero and formally negative, with a residual r.m.s. of 2.8 pc cm−3. We do not quote the implied upper limit as a constraint on the cluster’s gas: a model whose residuals exceed its measurement errors by three orders of magnitude is falsified by its own fit, and the formal error bar of a falsified model is not a credible interval. What the fit does establish is the requirement. At the present per-sight-line scatter, a 3σ determination of ne = 0.1 cm−3 needs the scatter modelled down by a factor of 7, or of order 103 sight lines at the current scatter, which no foreseeable timing programme will deliver. The tractable path is the former: the excess scatter is structure, in the Galactic foreground or in the cluster, and it is measurable in its own right through the DM derivatives that five of these pulsars already have. Anderson (1993) attributed the M15 dispersion-measure gradient across its seven core pulsars to the foreground interstellar medium on the same evidence of no radial dependence within the core.

8. Scope of the adjudication

Epistemic status: Sections 2–7 are instrument-level results and standard accretion arithmetic; nothing in them depends on any hypothesis of this paper set. This section prices the result against the two live readings and is interpretive.

Under the null reading, the center is gas-starved or its flow is suppressed. Under the efficiency law adopted here it pays a price everywhere in the contested range: at each of the three anchors it must give up one of its own ingredients, the mass, the fiducial density, or the standard suppression bracket. That is a constraint on conventional astrophysics, publishable and testable on its own terms, and it no longer sharpens the mass tension from one side only, since electromagnetic silence is now hard to buy naturally across the whole range the kinematics argue over. The density is the ingredient this paper’s own Section 4 makes it easiest to give up, and doing so is the cheapest way to restore the null reading. Under the engineered reading of Papers A and E, a system extracting accretion power as work rather than radiation presents a deep multi-band null as its expected signature, at any mass; the data cannot separate that reading from the null, and this paper does not claim otherwise. What it contributes to that adjudication is the currency: a likelihood over εB with stated conditioning, which Paper E’s framework ingests in place of the single-band, single-convention numbers it previously had to translate. The duty-cycle loophole of Section 6 is charged to both readings equally.

9. Conclusion

The deepest radio, infrared, and X-ray observations of ω Cen’s center jointly admit a posterior on the Bondi radiative efficiency of any central accretion flow: ε95 = 3.0×10−10 at the fast-star mass anchor for a RIAF-like flow under the stated priors, 1.4×10−8 with no radio information, and a further factor of 27 tighter at the top of the contested mass range. Scored against a natural outflow-suppressed hot flow at the fiducial density, the data reject 85, 77 and 99 per cent of that flow’s parameter space at the fast-star anchor, the pulsar-timing cap and 4×104 M☉, and above 0.57, 0.47 and 0.93 across every density prior and convention of Table 6. The curve’s normalization is owned by the unmeasured central density, and the cluster’s own stellar winds now estimate that density well below the analogy value: a recount of the giants inside the influence radius gives eight of them and a no-removal steady state near 0.03 cm−3 at the assumed outflow speed, with 0.03–0.08 cm−3 across the estimates of Section 4, a factor of three to eight under the 47 Tucanae value the prior is centred on. That recount points the density evidence towards the weaker end of the exclusion range even as the efficiency correction points the verdict towards the stronger. A pulsar dispersion-measure gradient would settle the density, but at the present sight-line scatter that fit is foreground-limited: the 19-pulsar set returns −0.33 ± 0.24 cm−3 against residuals about 103 times the measurement errors, and a 3σ determination needs the scatter modelled down by a factor of 7, or of order 103 sight lines (Section 7). Until that scatter is modelled, the density stays an analogy-conditional input. In the meantime, Figure 1 is the quantitative meaning of ω Cen’s silence, and the engineered-silence hypothesis of this paper set lives inside it on equal terms with a gas-starvation null that must now give up one of its own ingredients at every anchor rather than only at the highest.

Appendix A. Conventions, unit conversions, and cross-paper reconciliation

Bondi rate. Eq. 1 uses the γ = 5/3 eigenvalue λ = 0.25 (Bondi 1952). A constant copied from the 1952 numerics belongs to the same error species as the ξ correction recorded in Paper H, so we state how it verifies. Bondi’s general polytropic eigenvalue, valid for 1 < γ < 5/3, is

λ(γ) = 1/4[2/(5−3γ)](5−3γ)/2(γ−1), (10)

which evaluates to 0.500 at γ = 1.5, 0.625 at γ = 1.4 and 1.120 as γ → 1, reproducing the tabulated values, while as γ → 5/3 the bracketed factor tends to unity and leaves the prefactor 1/4 (see also Frank et al. 2002). Up to v1.5 this equation was printed with a spurious second factor (Appendix C); it is removed here, the values above are recomputed in figs/fF_r9_checks.py, and no number elsewhere in this paper used it: ṀB was always evaluated at λ = 0.25. At γ = 5/3 itself the regular sonic point of the general solution merges with the origin, so the value is read as this limit rather than from a critical-point evaluation. The rate uses a gas-rest-frame denominator cs3, with cs drawn between the μ = 0.6 isothermal value (11.7 km s−1) and the pure-hydrogen adiabatic value (16.6 km s−1) at 104 K. Paper E’s fuel budget (its Eq. 2) uses λ = 1 with a (σ2 + cs2)3/2 denominator as a turbulent-medium convention; the net ratio between the two conventions is 0.25 (σ2 + cs2)3/2/cs3, which is 1.43 at the median sound speed cs = 14.15 km s−1 and reaches 1.9 only at cs ≃ 12.3 km s−1, the 13th percentile of the prior. The 1.9 figure and ṀB = 5.3×1018 g s−1 at 2×104 M☉ quoted as median values in v1.5 both belong to that 13th-percentile sound speed. At the median the rate is ṀB = 3.5×1018 g s−1 against Paper E’s 3×1018, so this paper’s ṀB is 1.4× Paper E’s at equal (M, ne) under median nuisances and its εB limits are correspondingly 1.4× tighter than under E’s convention. Both statements are correct within their stated conventions; the correction is to the exchange rate, not to either paper’s own numbers.

Electron mean molecular weight. μe = 2/(1 + X) = 1.17 at X = 0.71.

Radio. LR ≡ νLν at 5 GHz under a flat spectrum: LR = 4πd2 (5 GHz) Sν, with Sν the 7.25 GHz flux density (Lν flat). At d = 5.49 kpc, 1.1 μJy corresponds to LR = 2.0×1026 erg s−1 (5.9×1026 at 3σ).

X-ray bands. The fundamental plane is calibrated on 2–10 keV; the Chandra limit is 0.5–7 keV; F(2–10)/F(0.5–7) = ln(10/2)/ln(7/0.5) = 0.610 for Γ = 2, which is the value used throughout. Haggard et al. (2013) derive their own limit under Γ = 2.3, and at that index the ratio of power-law integrals is 0.462, lower by 24 per cent. The radio leg enters as LR ∝ (kfXLbol)0.60, so the change moves the predicted radio luminosity by 0.85 and loosens the RIAF anchors by 15 to 20 per cent (Table 10), the largest single convention effect in this appendix. It is carried as a ladder row rather than adopted, because the plane’s own calibration sample is not restricted to Γ = 2.3 sources and changing k alone would mix the two calibrations.

The mismatch against the 5.49 kpc prior shifts the X-ray leg by nothing: the X-ray likelihood is evaluated on flux, which is what Chandra measured and is distance-independent, so the drawn distance enters the predicted flux and never the limit.
